## Week 2 | Business Process Management

#### Business Process Management (BPM) is the discipline of designing, analysing, improving, and governing end‑to‑end business processes so organisations can operate efficiently, consistently, and strategically. At its core, BPM treats processes as business assets—not just tasks—requiring clear ownership, measurable performance, and continuous optimisation.

📌 **Source:** Chen, L, Feng, Y & Wang, C 2025, 'Revisiting the core principles of business process management: A contemporary perspective', Business Process Management Journal, vol. 31, no. 1, pp. 78-95.

This scholarly article revisits the core principles of Business Process Management in the context of contemporary organisational challenges and technological advancements. The authors examine nine key principles: managed continuously in a life cycle model, a journey rather than a destination, about people as much as process, involves all relevant stakeholders, approached from the outside in (customer-driven), managed holistically, aligned with corporate strategy, performance-driven management discipline, and segments primary cross-functional processes that deliver value. The research evaluates how these principles apply in digital transformation contexts and identifies emerging challenges in their implementation.

I selected this artefact because it directly addresses the Week 2 lecture's coverage of core BPM principles. The lecture introduced these principles as fundamental to understanding BPM as a disciplined management approach (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 40). This 2025 article demonstrates how these principles remain relevant while also evolving with changing business environments. The article's emphasis on customer-driven approaches and alignment with corporate strategy reinforces the lecture's discussion of the BPM-digital value framework and the importance of value-driven transformation (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 53). This artefact demonstrates my understanding that BPM is a holistic discipline that requires consideration of people, processes, and technology, and that it must be approached as an ongoing journey rather than a destination.

**Strategy Maps and Value-Driver Trees in BPM**
Artefact Type: Video Tutorial
Source: BPM-D 2025, 'Creating Strategy Maps and Value-Driver Trees for Process Excellence', BPM-D Insights, 10 March, viewed [date], https://www.bpm-d.com.

**Summary**

This video tutorial explains how organisations can develop strategy maps and value-driver trees to connect business strategy to process improvement initiatives. The tutorial demonstrates how strategy maps visually represent organisational objectives across financial, customer, internal process, and learning and growth perspectives. Value-driver trees are then used to break down strategic goals into measurable value drivers and key performance indicators. The tutorial includes practical examples of how these tools have been applied in various industries to achieve strategy-driven process design and improvement.

**Justification**

I chose this artefact because it provides a practical, visual explanation of strategy maps and value-driver trees, which were key topics in the Week 2 lecture. The lecture explained that value-driver trees derive strategic value-drivers from the organisation's strategy and that strategy maps provide a framework for aligning business processes with strategic objectives (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 64). This 2025 tutorial demonstrates how these tools are used in practice to create a strategic execution plan, reinforcing the lecture's emphasis on moving strategy into execution quickly and with low risk (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 53). The artefact supports my understanding that BPM is fundamentally about translating strategy into operational performance through well-designed and managed processes.

##  Porter's Value Chain Analysis in Modern Organisations
Artefact Type: eBook
Source: Thompson, R 2025, Value Chain Analysis in the Digital Era, Routledge, London.

**Summary**

This book explores how Porter's Value Chain Analysis remains relevant in modern organisations despite the digital transformation of business operations. The author examines how primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service) and support activities (firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, procurement) can be analysed and optimised using contemporary BPM techniques. The book also discusses how digital technologies have transformed value chains and provides frameworks for mapping digital value chains in modern organisations.

**Justification**

I selected this artefact because it addresses the Week 2 lecture's coverage of Porter's Value Chain Analysis as a tool for identifying and segmenting business processes (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 66). The lecture explained that primary/core processes (20%) directly deliver value to customers, support processes (70%) support primary processes, and management processes (10%) measure, monitor, and control business activities (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 45). This 2025 book demonstrates how value chain analysis remains a fundamental tool for BPM professionals to identify high-impact processes and prioritise improvement initiatives. This artefact supports my understanding of how process types are categorised and how organisations can identify the processes that create the most value.

## Impact Assessment and Process Prioritisation
Artefact Type: Case Study
Source: 'Strategic process prioritisation: A case study in financial services' 2025, Journal of Process Management, vol. 18, no. 4, pp. 112-128.


**Summary**
This case study examines how a financial services organisation conducted an impact assessment to identify and prioritise high-impact processes for transformation. The study describes the methodology used to assess processes against criteria including customer-facing nature, revenue impact, strategic alignment, and cross-functional coordination requirements. The findings demonstrate that organisations must systematically evaluate processes based on strategic value and business impact to ensure effective resource allocation in BPM initiatives.

**Justification**
I chose this artefact because it demonstrates the practical application of impact assessment and process prioritisation, which were covered in the Week 2 lecture. The lecture explained that organisations should identify high-impact processes based on criteria including customer-facing processes, high impact on revenue, alignment to other high-value processes, and critical cross-functional coordination (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 56). This 2025 case study demonstrates how these principles are applied in a real organisational context, providing evidence that systematic process prioritisation is essential for effective BPM. This artefact supports my understanding of the BPM life cycle framework and the importance of phase 1: process identification and prioritisation aligned with strategy.
