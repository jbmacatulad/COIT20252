# E-Portfolio 2 – Business Process Modelling


**Week 3 | What is Process Analysis?**

Process analysis is the first stage of Phase 2 in the BPM Life Cycle Framework (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 130). It is defined as the systematic examination of all process-related activities to measure the operations and effectiveness of those activities in meeting the organisation's goals (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 130).

According to the BPM CBOK® Guide, a process is "a defined set of sequential or parallel activities that achieve a particular, definable, measurable result" (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 130). In the context of BPM, a business process is understood as an end-to-end set of activities that delivers a product or service to a customer.

In simpler terms, process analysis is the practice of understanding how work gets done, why it gets done that way, and whether it is achieving the desired outcomes.

**Why is Process Analysis Conducted?**
Process analysis serves several critical purposes in organisations (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 131):
<br>

| Purpose | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- |
| Evaluate efficiency | Assess how efficiently the organisation is working to meet its objectives |
| Create shared understanding | Develop a common view of how work currently gets done |
| Identify improvement opportunities | Redesign processes to better meet organisational goals |
| Identify obsolete processes | Recognise processes that no longer add value |
| Inform strategic decisions | Enable informed decisions about adapting to changing environments |


**Process analysis can also address specific improvement opportunities including:**

> More efficient operations

> Avoidance and/or mitigation of risks

> Potential blended operations between two organisations

> Impact analysis of pending regulations

(ABPMP International, 2019, p. 131)

<br><br> 

**When is Process Analysis Conducted?**
Process analysis can be triggered by various events (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 133):

| Trigger | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| Continuous Monitoring | Ongoing evaluation as part of regular operations |
| Event-Triggered | Specific events such as customer complaints or process failures |
| Strategic Planning | As part of strategic reviews and planning cycles |
| Performance Issues | When processes are not meeting performance targets |
| New Technologies | When new technologies create opportunities for improvement |
| Merger, Acquisition, or Divestiture | When organisational structures change |
| Regulatory Requirements | When new regulations require process changes |

<br>

**Methods and Tools for Process Analysis**
Process analysis employs a variety of methods and tools to gather information and evaluate processes. These can be categorised into information gathering methods and analytical methods.

Information Gathering Methods
The BPM CBOK® Guide identifies the following methods for gathering information during process analysis (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 143):


| Method | Description | When to Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Direct Observation | Observing employees performing the process in real-time | When you need to see actual behaviour versus documented procedures |
| One-on-One Interviews | Speaking individually with process participants and stakeholders | When you need in-depth understanding from key individuals |
| Written Feedback | Collecting written responses through surveys or questionnaires | When you need input from a large number of people |
| Structured Workshops | Facilitating group sessions to gather insights from multiple stakeholders | When you need collaborative problem-solving and consensus |
| Web Conferencing | Remote meetings to gather information from distributed teams | When stakeholders are geographically dispersed |
| Process Mining | Using event logs and data to discover and analyse actual process behaviour | When you need objective, data-driven insights |
| Researching Existing Documentation | Reviewing process manuals, policies, and existing models | When you need historical context and documented procedures |

### Artefact 1 – Quality and Accuracy in BPMN Modelling

**Artefact Type:** Scholarly journal article
**Source:** Hörner, Möller and Reichert (2026)
<img width="627" height="416" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1b433ba0-88d9-46a1-be83-643548d0fbf9" />


Hörner, Möller and Reichert (2026, p. 59) investigate BPMNGen, an AI-based framework that converts natural-language process descriptions into BPMN 2.0 models. Their study evaluates whether generated models are semantically accurate and understandable. The results show that automated modelling performs well for simpler processes but becomes less reliable as complexity increases.
I chose this artefact because it changed how I judge process-model quality. Previously, I focused mainly on whether the symbols and sequence looked technically correct. The study made me realise that a useful model must also represent the intended business situation accurately and remain understandable to stakeholders. This is meaningful evidence of my learning because I now see modelling quality as both technical correctness and clear communication, not simply a visually correct diagram.

### Artefact 2 – My BPMN Model: Enrol in a University Unit

**Artefact Type:** Own work – BPMN swimlane model
**Tool:** Bizagi Modeler, 2026

<img width="837" height="472" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7071e002-6f20-4bf6-ab87-aaed09354c9d" />



I created a BPMN swimlane model in Bizagi Modeler for the process **“Enrol in a University Unit.”** The model separates responsibilities between the Student, Student Portal/System and Course Management, and includes start and end events, tasks, sequence flows and gateways for prerequisite approval and class availability.

Creating the model helped me understand why BPMN is more useful than simply listing process steps. Hörner, Möller and Reichert (2026, p. 61) explain that BPMN models can represent tasks, decision points, resources and flows. Assigning activities to different lanes made responsibilities, handoffs and alternative outcomes clearer. The gateways also forced me to consider what happens when normal conditions are not met. This artefact demonstrates that I can apply modelling concepts to a familiar process rather than only explain BPMN theoretically.

### Artefact 3 – BPMN and Process Simulation

**Artefact Type:** Scholarly conference article
**Source:** Cimino et al. (2025)

<img width="530" height="410" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e2e42468-5ac1-4afc-bf56-ee00ce8d31fa" />


Cimino et al. (2025, p. 3236) examine how integrating simulation with BPMN allows process models to be tested under different conditions. Their study explains that simulation supports repeated process iterations and provides insights into potential improvements, resource management and organisational efficiency.

I selected this artefact because it corrected my assumption that completing a BPMN diagram means the modelling work is finished. I now understand that a model can also be used as an experimental tool. Simulation allows analysts to explore how a process could behave before changing the real operation and compare scenarios without immediately disrupting employees or customers. This is meaningful to my understanding of BPM because validation adds evidence to process design decisions. A model should not only look logical; its expected performance should also be tested where possible.

### Artefact 4 – BPMN Supporting Process Improvement

**Artefact Type:** Scholarly case study
**Source:** Costa and Ávila (2025)

<img width="428" height="172" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/6240f4c6-8cb6-4c0f-9316-cb7505075bb0" />

Costa and Ávila (2025, p. 6) demonstrate how BPMN supported improvement in a wind-blade manufacturing case by modelling existing work, identifying handoffs and redistributing responsibilities. After improvement actions, cycle times at three production positions decreased by approximately 27%, 7% and 20% respectively (Costa & Ávila 2025, p. 11).

I chose this artefact because it connects process modelling with redesign and performance measurement. My main learning was that a **to-be** model should not simply appear cleaner than the **as-is** model. The redesign should address an identified problem and its effect should be measurable. Seeing actual cycle-time reductions made the value of modelling more concrete. This artefact demonstrates my understanding that modelling, redesign and measurement should work together as part of continuous improvement rather than being treated as separate BPM activities.
