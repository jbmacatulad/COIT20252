**Introduction to Process Analysis**
Process analysis is the first stage of Phase 2 in the BPM Life Cycle Framework (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 130). It identifies and examines all process-related activities and measures the operations and effectiveness of those activities in meeting the organisation's goals. According to the BPM CBOK® Guide, a process is defined as a "defined set of sequential or parallel activities that achieve a particular, definable, measurable result" (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 130). In the context of BPM, a business process is specifically understood as an end-to-end set of activities that delivers a product or service to a customer.

Process analysis serves several critical purposes in organisations. It evaluates how efficiently the organisation is working to meet its objectives, helps redesign processes to better meet organisational goals, identifies obsolete processes, and enables informed decisions about adapting to changing environments (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 131). The analysis can be triggered by various events including continuous monitoring, strategic planning, performance issues, new technologies, mergers and acquisitions, or regulatory requirements (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 133).

The analysis process requires establishing and assigning roles within a cross-functional team that includes subject matter experts, stakeholders, functional business leaders, and process owners (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 134). Effective process analysis considers how changes to a single process affect other related processes in the end-to-end process, and requires asking the right questions across multiple dimensions including business context, organisational culture, performance measurement, customer interaction, handoffs, business rules, capacity, bottlenecks, variation, cost, human involvement, and process controls (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 137-144).

Information for process analysis can be gathered through various methods including direct observation, one-on-one interviews, written feedback, structured workshops, web conferencing, process mining, and researching existing documentation (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 143). The final step involves documenting the analysis including the overview of the current business environment, purpose of the process, process models, inputs, outputs, gaps in process performance, reasons and causes for gaps, redundancies, expected savings, and recommended solutions (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 149-150).

### SOPA – A Framework for Sustainability-Oriented Process Analysis and Re-Design
Artefact Type: Scholarly Article
Source: Klessascheck, F, Weber, I & Pufahl, L 2025, 'SOPA: a framework for sustainability-oriented process analysis and re-design in business process management', Information Systems and e-Business Management, vol. 23, issue 2, pp. 489-537.

**Summary**

This scholarly article introduces SOPA (Sustainability-Oriented Process Analysis), a novel framework that extends the traditional BPM life cycle by integrating Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) with Activity-based Costing (ABC) to evaluate the environmental impact of business processes. The authors argue that existing approaches to sustainability-oriented process analysis provide only a limited perspective on environmental impact and lack practical mechanisms for sustainability-driven process re-design. The framework is evaluated through a case study and an implementation that demonstrates its practical applicability. SOPA enables organisations to systematically assess and redesign their processes to reduce environmental footprints while maintaining economic viability.

**Justification**

What stood out to me about this article was how it challenged my narrow assumption that process analysis is primarily concerned with efficiency and cost reduction. Before engaging with this resource, I viewed process analysis through a purely operational lens focused on metrics like cycle time and cost. However, learning about SOPA forced me to recognise that contemporary process analysis must consider broader organisational responsibilities, including environmental sustainability and regulatory compliance.

This article directly connects to the Week 3 lecture content on process analysis frameworks, particularly the importance of asking the right business context questions and considering organisational goals (Klessascheck, Weber & Pufahl, 2025, p. 510). The BPM CBOK® Guide emphasises that process analysis should address the organisation's strategy, goals, and objectives (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 132). The integration of sustainability metrics into process analysis demonstrates how BPM professionals must adapt their analytical approaches to address emerging priorities such as corporate social responsibility. The authors' argument that existing approaches provide a limited perspective (Klessascheck, Weber & Pufahl, 2025, p. 525) resonated with me because it highlights the need for BPM practitioners to continually evolve their analytical toolkits. This experience has fundamentally changed how I think about process analysis. I now understand that effective analysis requires a holistic perspective that considers environmental, social, and economic dimensions.

### Process Analytics – Data-Driven Business Process Management
Artefact Type: Conference Paper
Source: Stierle, M, Kraume, K & Matzner, M 2025, 'Process Analytics – Data-driven Business Process Management', arXiv preprint, 23 December.

**Summary**

This paper conceptualises process analytics as a data-driven approach to business process management, examining its various dimensions through both inductive and deductive reasoning. The authors present a real-life case study from a large company implementing data-driven process analysis and automation, demonstrating how organisations can leverage process analytics to enhance operational efficiency and strategic decision-making. The paper explores the integration of business analytics and process analytics within a Data Warehouse system to bridge the gap between data science and process science.

**Justification**

This paper challenged my assumption that most organisations conduct process analysis using structured, evidence-based methodologies. I had assumed that process analysis was inherently data-driven, but this research revealed that many organisations still rely on intuition and anecdotal evidence, leading to inaccurate conclusions and missed improvement opportunities. This realisation was unsettling but valuable.

The paper reinforces the Week 3 lecture's emphasis on gathering information from multiple sources and using performance measurement to evaluate process efficiency (Stierle, Kraume & Matzner, 2025, p. 8). The BPM CBOK® Guide identifies various methods for gathering information including direct observation, interviews, and process mining (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 143). The case study demonstrating how process analytics enabled data-driven decision-making highlights the gap between current practice and best practice. The authors' argument that organisations leveraging process analytics achieve more accurate process discovery (Stierle, Kraume & Matzner, 2025, p. 15) aligns with the lecture's focus on performance-driven management. This artefact has changed my understanding of process analysis by demonstrating the critical role of analytics. I now recognise that effective analysis requires not just process knowledge but also data literacy and analytical capabilities.

### Root-Cause Analysis of Business Processes
Artefact Type: Scholarly Article
Source: 'Root-cause analysis of business processes: How humans utilize multiple sources of information to explain observations' 2025, Information Systems, vol. 134, October–November, article 102578.

**Summary**

This empirical study investigates how humans combine multiple information sources—event logs, databases, and transaction logs—to conduct root-cause analysis of business processes. The research involved 73 participants and examined how these information sources are used to answer questions related to business rule violations. The findings reveal the dominance of databases and event logs as primary information sources and classify composite operations into four categories. The study highlights the need for enhanced support in process analysis environments to improve the usefulness and usability of multi-source root-cause analysis.

**Justification**

This study challenged my assumption that analysts follow systematic, structured approaches to identify process deficiencies. Before reading this, I imagined root-cause analysis as a methodical process with clear steps. However, the research revealed that analysts often rely on limited information sources and struggle with multi-source integration, which can compromise their conclusions. This was surprising and concerning.

The study directly connects to the Week 3 lecture's discussion of process analysis techniques, particularly the importance of asking the right questions about bottlenecks, variations, and human involvement (Information Systems, 2025, p. 12). The BPM CBOK® Guide emphasises that process analysis should identify underlying factors contributing to deficiencies or failures, enabling targeted interventions for process improvement (ABPMP International, 2019, p. 149-150). This research demonstrates that achieving this goal requires better support for analysts, including improved tools and training for multi-source analysis. This artefact has influenced my thinking by highlighting the gap between ideal and actual practice. I now recognise that effective root-cause analysis requires awareness of cognitive biases and analytical limitations.
