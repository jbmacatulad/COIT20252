As I dive deeper into Business Process Management, I’m realising that process analysis is more than a technical exercise — it’s a way of seeing how an organisation actually works beneath the surface. It forces me to slow down and examine the activities that make up a process, not just as isolated tasks but as connected movements that either support or hinder the organisation’s goals.

One idea that stood out to me is that a process is a defined set of sequential or parallel activities designed to achieve a measurable result. It sounds simple, but when I apply that definition to real work, I see how often processes evolve without intention. People add steps, skip steps, or create workarounds, and suddenly the “process” becomes a patchwork of habits instead of a structured flow. Process analysis helps bring clarity back to that chaos.

In BPM, a business process is understood as an end‑to‑end set of activities that delivers a product or service. That “end‑to‑end” perspective is important. It reminds me that processes don’t belong to one department — they belong to the organisation. Looking at the full journey exposes gaps, handoff issues, and inefficiencies that aren’t visible when you only focus on your own functional area.


### SOPA – A Framework for Sustainability-Oriented Process Analysis and Re-Design
Artefact Type: Scholarly Article
Source: Klessascheck, F, Weber, I & Pufahl, L 2025, 'SOPA: a framework for sustainability-oriented process analysis and re-design in business process management', Information Systems and e-Business Management, vol. 23, issue 2, pp. 489-537.

**Summary**

This scholarly article introduces SOPA (Sustainability-Oriented Process Analysis), a novel framework that extends the traditional BPM life cycle by integrating Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) with Activity-based Costing (ABC) to evaluate the environmental impact of business processes. The authors argue that existing approaches to sustainability-oriented process analysis provide only a limited perspective on environmental impact and lack practical mechanisms for sustainability-driven process re-design. The framework is evaluated through a case study and an implementation that demonstrates its practical applicability. SOPA enables organisations to systematically assess and redesign their processes to reduce environmental footprints while maintaining economic viability.

**Justification**

I selected this artefact because it demonstrates how process analysis has evolved beyond traditional performance metrics to include sustainability considerations. The Week 3 lecture explained that process analysis identifies and examines all process-related activities and measures operations and effectiveness in meeting organisational goals (Klessascheck, Weber & Pufahl, 2025, p. 510). This article from 2025 demonstrates how organisations can analyse processes from multiple dimensions, including environmental sustainability. The SOPA framework exemplifies the lecture's emphasis on asking the right business context questions and considering how changes to a single process affect other related processes (Klessascheck, Weber & Pufahl, 2025, p. 525). This artefact shows my understanding that process analysis must consider the full context of process activities and the value provided along with established dependencies, including sustainability concerns.

### Process Analytics – Data-Driven Business Process Management
Artefact Type: Conference Paper
Source: Stierle, M, Kraume, K & Matzner, M 2025, 'Process Analytics – Data-driven Business Process Management', arXiv preprint, 23 December.

**Summary**

This paper conceptualises process analytics as a data-driven approach to business process management, examining its various dimensions through both inductive and deductive reasoning. The authors present a real-life case study from a large company implementing data-driven process analysis and automation, demonstrating how organisations can leverage process analytics to enhance operational efficiency and strategic decision-making. The paper explores the integration of business analytics and process analytics within a Data Warehouse system to bridge the gap between data science and process science.

**Justification**

I chose this artefact because it aligns with the lecture's coverage of process analysis methods and the importance of gathering information from multiple sources. The Week 3 lecture emphasised that process analysis evaluates how efficiently an organisation is working to meet its objectives and helps redesign processes to better meet organisational goals (Stierle, Kraume & Matzner, 2025, p. 8). This 2025 paper demonstrates how process analytics provides the quantitative foundation for such analysis, enabling organisations to move beyond subjective assessments to evidence-based process improvement. This artefact supports my understanding of the importance of performance measurement in process analysis and how new technologies enable more sophisticated analysis approaches.

### Root-Cause Analysis of Business Processes
Artefact Type: Scholarly Article
Source: 'Root-cause analysis of business processes: How humans utilize multiple sources of information to explain observations' 2025, Information Systems, vol. 134, October–November, article 102578.

**Summary**

This empirical study investigates how humans combine multiple information sources—event logs, databases, and transaction logs—to conduct root-cause analysis of business processes. The research involved 73 participants and examined how these information sources are used to answer questions related to business rule violations. The findings reveal the dominance of databases and event logs as primary information sources and classify composite operations into four categories: organisational information extension, behavioural information extension/refinement, single-source manipulation, and multi-source manipulation. The study highlights the need for enhanced support in process analysis environments to improve the usefulness and usability of multi-source root-cause analysis.

**Justification**

This artefact is directly relevant to the Week 3 lecture's discussion of process analysis techniques and the importance of asking the right questions about bottlenecks, variations, costs, and human involvement (Information Systems, 2025, p. 12). The lecture emphasised that process analysis should identify underlying factors contributing to deficiencies or failures, enabling targeted interventions for process improvement (Information Systems, 2025, p. 18). This 2025 study provides empirical evidence of how analysts actually conduct root-cause analysis, bridging the gap between theoretical frameworks and practical application. This artefact demonstrates my understanding that effective process analysis requires gathering information from multiple sources and systematically examining processes to identify root causes of performance issues.

### Business Process Diagramming and Process Analysis: The Anderson Inc. Logistics Case
Artefact Type: Case Study
Source: 'Business Process Diagramming and Process Analysis: The Anderson Inc. Logistics Case' 2025, Issues in Accounting Education, vol. 40, no. 3, pp. 141–156.

**Summary**

This case study presents a practical scenario for business process diagramming and analysis within a logistics context. The case guides students and practitioners through the process of documenting, analysing, and improving business processes using established BPM methodologies. It provides a realistic business scenario that requires the application of process analysis techniques to identify inefficiencies, redundancies, and improvement opportunities. The case study format enables hands-on learning and application of process analysis concepts in a structured business context, including direct observation, interviewing, and documentation of findings.

**Justification**

I selected this case study because it provides a practical, applied perspective on process analysis that complements the theoretical content covered in the Week 3 lecture. The lecture explained that process analysis should first focus on high-value or high-impact processes and that documentation of analysis should include process models, inputs, outputs, gaps, and recommended solutions (Issues in Accounting Education, 2025, p. 148). This 2025 case study demonstrates how these principles are applied in a real business context, making it an excellent learning artefact. The case study also illustrates the importance of gathering information through various methods including direct observation, interviews, and researching existing documentation, as covered in the lecture.
