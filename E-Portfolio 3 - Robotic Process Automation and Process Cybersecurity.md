# E-Portfolio 3 – Robotic Process Automation and Process Cybersecurity

Robotic Process Automation (RPA) uses software bots to automate repetitive and rule-based activities that would otherwise be completed manually. Process cybersecurity focuses on protecting business processes and their supporting systems while maintaining confidentiality, integrity and availability (CQUniversity 2026, p. 24). Together, these topics highlight an important BPM issue: automation can improve process speed and consistency, but increased dependence on digital systems also creates new risks that must be identified, controlled and continuously monitored.

### Artefact 1 – RPA in Procurement Transformation

**Artefact Type:** Scholarly industry case study  
<br>

**Figure 1.** RPA process used in the procurement function of the case organisation.
Source: Picoli and Bin (2025, p. 7).
**Source:** Picoli and Bin (2025)

<br><br>

Picoli and Bin (2025, p. 7) examine RPA adoption in the procurement function of a large multinational organisation. Rather than automating all purchasing activities, the organisation selected low-complexity, highly specified tasks to reduce buyers’ operational workload. The study considers the implementation from financial, process and people perspectives.

What stood out to me was how limited the robot’s scope actually was. I previously associated successful automation with automating as much repetitive work as possible. Instead, the case shows that careful task selection is critical. This changed my view of RPA from being mainly a productivity tool to being a process-design decision. A bot may be technically capable of performing a task, but that does not automatically make the task a suitable candidate for automation.

### Artefact 2 – Critical Success Factors for RPA

**Artefact Type:** Scholarly journal article  

<br><br>

<img width="463" height="524" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/dfbdf013-b679-453b-b3ac-2da177e1cf71" />

**Figure 2.** Critical success factors across the RPA adoption lifecycle.
**Source:** Ge et al. (2025)

<br><br>

Ge et al. examine RPA adoption in the hotel industry and identify factors influencing implementation across its lifecycle. Before implementation, organisations need clear goals, appropriate process selection and stakeholder alignment. During and after implementation, process standardisation, monitoring, performance measurement and continued training become important (Ge et al. 2025, pp. 7–9).

What stood out to me was that the difficult question is not simply **“Can we automate this task?”** but **“Is this process ready to be automated?”** I had previously associated repetitive work with being automatically suitable for RPA. The study showed that an inconsistent process or weak stakeholder support can undermine a technically capable bot. This made me view RPA as an organisational and process-management decision rather than purely a technology decision.

### Artefact 3 – Australia’s Cyber Threat Environment

**Artefact Type:** Australian Government industry report  

<br><br>

<img width="352" height="166" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8b7a1c41-74fa-4cd6-b08e-ad0ed50a33e0" />

**Figure 3.** Key findings and cybersecurity priorities from Australia's 2024–25 cyber threat environment.
**Source:** Australian Signals Directorate (2025)

<br><br>

The Australian Signals Directorate’s *Annual Cyber Threat Report 2024–25* demonstrates the scale of risk surrounding increasingly digital business processes. ASD’s Australian Cyber Security Centre responded to more than 1,200 cybersecurity incidents during the reporting period and advises organisations to operate with an **“assume compromise”** mindset while strengthening logging, legacy technology management and third-party risk controls (ASD 2025, p. 1).

The idea of assuming compromise changed how I think about process cybersecurity. I previously viewed successful cybersecurity mainly as preventing attackers from gaining access. I now see resilience as equally important. An automated process must be monitored, recoverable and controllable when prevention fails. This is particularly relevant to RPA because bots rely on credentials, applications and connected data, meaning automation can create new exposure at the same time as it creates efficiency.

### Artefact 4 – My Cybersecurity Risk and Control Matrix

**Artefact Type:** Own work – cybersecurity risk assessment  
**Context:** XYZ University Enrolment System, 2026 (XYZ University hypothetical case study from Week 8 CQU Lecture)

<br><br>

<img width="425" height="235" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/04a23c86-a9bf-4103-bbc9-a1b46840d94f" />

**Figure 4.** Cybersecurity risk and control assessment for the XYZ University enrolment process.
**Source:** Own work (2026).

<br><br>

I developed a cybersecurity risk-and-control matrix for the hypothetical XYZ University enrolment system. I assessed risks including unpatched software, compromised credentials, excessive privileges, ransomware and third-party compromise against their likelihood, impact and effect on confidentiality, integrity and availability. Current Australian guidance recommends controls including application and operating-system patching, multi-factor authentication, restricted administrative privileges and regular backups (ASD 2025, p. 44).

Creating the matrix made cybersecurity feel less like a technical checklist and more like a BPM design issue. I noticed that security controls involve trade-offs. Stronger authentication protects confidential information but can introduce additional steps for users, while availability controls must protect services without obstructing legitimate access. My main learning is that cybersecurity should be designed around the consequences of process failure rather than added only after automation has been implemented.
